The Competency Dictionary

A practical guide to the behaviors that contribute to outstanding leader development in early childhood education: what each competency is, how it deepens through levels, and how it sounds in the words of practicing leaders.

Introduction

Strong leadership lets educators, families, and children thrive

The Early Years Leadership Diversity Initiative conducted in-depth interviews with 17 ECE leaders who supported the professional growth of a rising leader. We set out to understand what they did and what helps or hinders them from being effective. To help us identify what worked to support effective leader development, we used the concept of competencies – the underlying characteristics that predict outstanding impact in a given situation.

We developed this dictionary to help early childhood leaders – and those who support them – to identify and cultivate the behaviors that contribute to outstanding leader development. Our research identified five core competencies associated with effective leader development in ECE.

Each competency exists across several levels that deepen in complexity as leaders progress in their understanding and practice of the competency. The levels are not intended to represent fixed categories of people. Rather, they provide a framework for understanding growth and recognizing that individuals may demonstrate different levels across different competencies, contexts, and stages of their careers.

How to use this dictionary

This dictionary is a developmental resource for current and aspiring early childhood leaders. Use it to:

  • Reflect on personal strengths and areas for growth
  • Guide coaching, mentoring, and professional development conversations
  • Support leadership development planning
  • Create a shared language for discussing effective leadership practices
  • Identify opportunities to help emerging leaders expand their capabilities and impact

The developmental levels are guideposts, not a checklist. Growth is rarely linear, and leadership effectiveness depends on context. The goal is not to label leaders, but to support continuous learning and provide a practical framework for developing the next generation of early childhood leaders.

GO Foundational competency · 6 levels Guiding Others (GO) Genuine intent to teach others and help them be successful in current and future roles.

This competency involves a genuine intent to teach others and help them be successful in current and future roles. This competency does NOT require a formal training role. Rather, it involves the intent to help another develop and outcomes related to development. Unlike Managing Performance, where the focus is on the task, this competency focuses on the intent to develop a person’s profession or career.

Key componentsDirective/advisoryLeader-Developer guides growthFocused on job/career successNon-cumulative
There are six levels to this competency.
Missed opportunity (−1)Fails to help others to be successful in their jobs
  • Ignores an opportunity to help someone be successful in the job, when the norm would be to help
Level 1Expresses belief or positive expectations that others want to develop
  • Makes positive comments about others’ capabilities to do a job well or progress in their career (comments can be made about or to the other person)
  • Believes that others want to grow in their careers and/or can learn to improve their performance

“She was the one taking notes, asking the critical questions or asking the questions no one had thought of and those types of things. So [I thought], ‘oh, I love it.’”

Level 2Gives focused, short-term instructions/suggestions for how to get a task done
  • Provides detailed instructions/checklists and/or on-the-job demonstrations
  • Makes helpful suggestions for how to get aspects of the job done
  • Asks questions/checks in for understanding if others know what they need to do

“We were going through step-by-step of the different pieces that are required for students to join the college, the different roles that she’ll have in terms of supporting them once they join the college and the logistics of how to create a program in a sense where she could imitate this every year as new cohorts come in.”

Level 3Provides individualized support/direction to help others be effective
  • Based on a needs-assessment, gives specific instructions to help someone be better in their current role
  • Gives individualized suggestions or direction for improvement
  • Makes time to help others as they are learning a new role
  • Provides context for the tasks at hand

It doesn’t have to be perfect. It’s okay to make mistakes.”

Level 4Supports development
  • For developmental purposes, gives specific positive or mixed feedback
  • Gives corrective feedback in behavioral, not personal, terms
  • Reassures and/or expresses positive expectations for future performance when giving corrective feedback
  • Provides guidance on how to think through job challenges
  • Provides room within current job to expand their skill sets
  • Sees and plans for longer term job potential

Level 5Supports career growth by providing counseling, resources, and advice
  • Arranges appropriate and helpful assignments or other experiences for the purpose of fostering a person’s learning and development
  • Provides career coaching and may identify/direct a person to other job opportunities that further their career
  • Supports/validates others (letters of recommendation/supporting promotions) in their career advancement at little to no personal risk

Level 6Develops systemic capability
  • Goes beyond teaching one individual to helping a person think about learning for a group
  • Helps the person that is being developed think about broad talent needs
  • Develops people to build broader organizational talent

UO Relational competency · 4 levels Understanding Others (UO) The extent to which people understand other people’s behaviors.

This competency measures the extent to which people understand other people. It is the ability to accurately read, hear and understand partly expressed or unspoken thoughts, feelings and concerns of others. It measures increasing complexity and depth of understanding as to why people act as they do. It is a competency that measures understanding, not action, which is usually covered by other competencies.

There are four levels to this competency.
Missed opportunity (−1)Fails to accurately understand others’ behaviors
  • Reads others’ behaviors without understanding them
  • Acts on assumptions about how others are feeling that are untested
  • Makes no effort to understand why people are acting the way that they are

“She really did not want my help. I really think her whole thing about my expectations being too high, I think she was intimidated by me. I think all those things made working with me and her somewhat stressful in a way it shouldn’t have been because all I really wanted was for her to be successful and to really be able to shine.”

Related quote from research
Level 1Recognizes emotion in others
  • Recognizes emotion by reading body language, facial expression, and/or tone of voice

“I could tell that she was really frustrated.”

Related quote from research
Level 2Recognizes emotion AND understands verbal content
  • Is able to understand BOTH what someone is saying AND the emotion (by reading body language, facial expression and/or tone of voice) that is raised by what they are saying

“If I didn’t know her, I wouldn’t necessarily know that she was stressed or not. And it took some time for me to read those cues to know if she was stressed or not. And over time she could tell me, ‘oh my gosh, I have such and such due.’ And she never asked for a different timeline, but I could tell when she was stressed and I would offer a different timeline, and she would accept that. I don’t know if she didn’t ask for a different timeline because she felt like she couldn’t, but she accepted it if I offered it.”

Level 3Understands behavior
  • Makes inferences/assumptions that go beyond the explicit content and emotion
  • Understands current, unexpressed or poorly expressed thoughts, concerns or feelings

Level 4Understands underlying meaning for behavior
  • Displays an in-depth understanding of the ongoing reasons for a person’s behavior or responses

BR Relational competency · 4 levels Building Relationships (BR) The intent and actions taken to build and nurture authentic relationships that create trust.

This competency measures depth of relationships that are built for the purposes of developing others. This competency describes the actions taken to build and nurture authentic relationships and also measures the level of trust that has been built. It relies on authentic relationships where leaders believe in the capabilities of the people that they are developing and endorse and support their ambitions. The opposite of this competency is building relationships for purposes of self-interest.

There are four levels to this competency.
Missed opportunity (−1)Fails to build/nurture established relationships
  • Despite efforts, unable to build a relationship
  • For a variety of reasons, fails to maintain a previously important relationship
  • Pulls back from a relationship when it is hard to maintain
  • Uses developmental relationships for own interests

“I can work with her and roll with the punches if she is being honest and direct with me, but when she isn’t, I am no longer interested in engaging.”

Related quote from research
Level 1Builds or maintains rapport with others
  • Engages in conversations to learn more about the interests/life/challenges of others
  • May include invitations to meet outside of work hours/venues
  • Connects personally on work related topics

“She loved coffee, so we would meet at Starbucks once a month and she would just update me on what she was doing. We were prepared for her role at the staff meeting. It was just a regular way for us to check in. And in that I would find out other things too.”

Related quote from research
Level 2Builds personal connections as a basis for relationships
  • Exchanges personal information to begin to build trust and connection
  • Invites others to talk about non-work related topics
  • Creates a safe environment for others to bring personal concerns into professional discussions
  • Able to connect around strongly held professional views
  • Keeps people with leadership potential “on their radar”

“We were [meeting] during the pandemic, so it wasn’t like I could see her and see the transition of her pregnancy in terms of being a couple months or seeing her young child in person. And then it was a great conversation point because we work in early childhood education and her experience being in the classroom and merging all these different things together, even though we worked in higher education and that’s not necessarily what we were doing in the current time, it just allowed us to have conversations about the things that we had experience in and the things that were happening in our lives to make it more personal.”

Related quote from research
Level 3Demonstrates empathy/creates space in developmental relationships
  • Sees situations from others’ perspectives and is able to empathize with them
  • Creates space for relationship and trust building
  • Focuses specifically on building confidence and trust
  • Reaches out (one time)

And I mean, I can even feel in me now that moment, I can remember how her voice got deeper. It was just really notable embodied experience of something got totally shifted.”

Level 4Is deeply vested in developmental relationships

HI Relational competency · 4 levels Honoring Identities (HI) The extent to which a leader can recognize and appreciate similarities and/or differences in identities.

This competency measures the extent to which a leader is able to recognize and appreciate similarities and/or differences in identities, whether they be racial, gender, age, demographic, socioeconomic, etc. At higher levels of this competency, leaders are able to use these insights to take action and/or change behavior.

There are four levels to this competency.
Missed opportunity (−1)Fails to accurately understand others’ identity/identity dynamics
  • Reads others’ identity without understanding them
  • Acts on assumptions about how others are feeling that are untested
  • Makes no effort to understand why people are acting the way that they are

“It mirrored everything that I had read in the papers at the time of not-for-profit organizations being like, why are all the 20 something and early 30 somethings suddenly saying that they need to be the leader of the organization now? And trying to set the full agenda and telling us that we’re terrible if that’s not what we’re doing.”

Related quote from research
Level 1Recognizes and names identity similarity/difference
  • Recognizes and appreciates similarities and differences in identity
  • Finds connections through identity

“I appreciate the ability to connect with another woman of color early in her career, similar backgrounds in terms of us being the only girls and our family, and also just being in that kind of leadership role in both our families but then in our sectors.”

Related quote from research
Level 2Demonstrates deeper understanding of the nuance of identity
  • Does not make assumptions about identity; does not see shared identity as a monolith
  • Appreciates difference; deeply understands both shared similarities and differences in identity
  • Pays attention to/concerned with how the dynamic will play out

“I was very worried about me being an East Asian woman holding space with another East Asian woman who has a different relationship with her racial identity.”

Related quote from research
Level 3Takes intentional action to specifically address identity
  • Seeks to create opportunities for those with shared and/or underrepresented identities; takes direct action

“As a black woman leading an organization, I am always going to look for opportunities for other young black women to be in similar roles as me. … But I think especially for me, I really want to make sure I’m providing space and opportunity for young women of color to have an opportunity to be at tables that we usually are not at.”

Level 4Changes the trajectory of an identity-related issue
  • Does not see identity as a barrier; helps to turn a perceived identity barrier into an asset

EO Most predictive competency · 5 levels Enabling Others (EO) Genuine intent to help others grow and achieve their personal and professional goals through the facilitation of development rather than the transfer of skills.

This competency involves a genuine intent to help others grow and achieve their personal and professional goals through the facilitation of development rather than the transfer of skills. This competency uses leadership coaching to recognize the leadership potential of others and facilitate the release of their potential through self-discovery. Coaching requires that the leader ‘check their own ego’ in service of helping others to be stronger and put the growth of the individual ahead of the accomplishment of the task. It includes endorsing the ambitions of others and sponsoring them in their growth.

Key componentsFacilitative/reflectiveLeader-Developer creates conditions for growthFocused on professional and personal growth
There are five levels to this competency.
Missed opportunity (−1)Puts own interests/values ahead of person being coached

Level 1Sees/believes that others have the potential to be leaders
  • Notices and affirms leadership potential (comments can be made to or about others)
  • Encourages people to take action to achieve their goals
  • Believes and values the need to develop others to be stronger
  • Keeps people “on their radar” whom they see as having high leadership potential

“I’ve done it with everybody that I have worked with who has stood out to me as really high potential and now have a wake of many people who I keep my eye on, who I send job opportunities to, who I’m checking in on, what are you thinking about? And I am a little mother hen about it. Yeah, I’ll bug you. I’ll tell you you’re not doing what you need to do. Chop chop.”

Level 2Understands the value of empowering others to grow as leaders
  • Uses language that honors those being coached
  • Models being open to new experiences and taking risks by sharing personal stories and examples

“I would tell her, always show up as your authentic self. That’s what the world needs. You were created to show up as your authentic self. So whatever that is, whoever that is, just do it. And I would tell her, I show up in my big earrings every day. Some people ask questions, I show up with my head wrap on and they want to know what is it or what am I. I’ve literally had those questions. Or I show up with my big hair. No, you cannot touch it. But yes, you can ask me questions.”

Level 3Uses coaching strategies that stimulate others to learn for themselves
  • Uses coaching strategies such as questioning, reflection, mirroring, and painting a picture to build capabilities
  • Able to make a balanced/objective assessment of another’s specific leadership strengths and weaknesses and use that as a basis for coaching

“When I was working with her, I considered myself just as holding space so she could hear herself. I asked some questions and we wondered together and she started to solve her own problems…And I got to just witness what’s happening. Then I asked her to just tell me more about that? What was it like for you to go through this experience or what was coming up for you? And I tried bringing things deeper. My role was basically noticing, naming, acknowledging and listening and then sometimes just pausing and saying nothing. And so I just super strongly believe in the sound of silence, which I didn’t like much when I was younger, but I totally see the beauty of the sound of silence that allows people to take the moment to share what’s coming up for them in that particular moment.”

Level 4Takes risks/leverages one’s social capital to further someone’s development (sponsorship)
  • Advocates for others or others’ ideas when there is some risk to do so
  • Uses own relationships/network/resources (e.g. power) to talk up or advocate for others
  • Positions others in places where they will take significant developmental steps

And it did feel risky to me in some ways. Not because I didn’t believe in her, but it was because I was just not quite sure how ready she would be to have these conversations.”

MP Also in the dictionary competency · 5 levels Managing Performance (MP) The intent to hold others accountable to a standard of performance.

This competency implies the intent to hold others accountable to a standard of performance. While not a “negative” competency, it has a directive element where the power of one’s own position or of one’s personality is used appropriately and effectively with the long-term good of the organization in mind. In Guiding Others, feedback with a positive or balanced intent focuses on the development of the person. This competency includes providing feedback where the intent is to ensure that a standard of performance is met, with the development needs of the individual clearly secondary.

There are five levels to this competency.
Missed opportunity (−1)Fails to address performance issues that warrant action
  • Fails to confront people because of concern for harmonious relationships or identity difference
Level 1Tells people how to get work done
  • Gives people basic directions to accomplish the task at hand
  • Makes needs and requirements clear

“I’m the kind of supervisor that I’m going to lay out your job. You know your job, you do your job. We check in once a month about your job, but I’m not going to nag you every day about different things.”

Related quote from research
Level 2Sets limits
  • Firmly says no to unreasonable requests
  • Sets limits for others’ performance or behavior

“As we were now really looking at more just consistent performance-based issues, then I think it started to look a little different. And at that point I was having to have some explicit conversations of this is the expectation of your role to be able to do these things.”

Related quote from research
Level 3Demands high performance
  • Demands high performance, including letting people know that they are not meeting expectations
  • May step in if others are not accomplishing the task
  • Insists on compliance with set expectations

“I heard that this is what’s going on. We can’t be doing that in the classroom. This is what I expect from you for next time. What do you think that we could do as a team? How can I help you get there?”

Level 4Monitors adherence to standards of performance
  • Issues clear warnings about the consequences of failing to meet performance standards
  • May make performance gaps public
