Methodology

How we did this research

We interviewed 17 early childhood leaders about times they helped a rising leader grow, then studied those stories to find the behaviors that made development most effective. Here is exactly who we talked to, what we asked, and how we analyzed what we heard.

At a Glance

The research in six facts

The question

What do experienced early childhood (ECE) leaders do to develop rising leaders, and what helps or hinders them from being effective?

Who we talked to

17 Leader-Developers: ECE leaders who had supported the professional growth of a rising leader, from 7 U.S. states.

How we asked

75-minute structured Behavioral Event Interviews, which walk through specific real situations rather than general opinions.

What we analyzed

34 stories of ECE leader development, two from each leader (one that went well, one more challenging), each rated for impact and examined for the behaviors involved.

When

Interviews took place March–July 2024. Analysis was completed, and the report published, in 2026.

What came out of it

Five competencies linked with effective leader development, and a Competency Dictionary describing each one.

Step by Step

From interviews to competencies

For the full technical detail, see Appendix A of the report (pages 27–28).

Six steps, from recruiting leaders to writing the dictionary.
1Step 1We recruited experienced leaders who had developed others

Participants were ECE leaders who had supported the professional growth of a rising leader. We call them Leader-Developers. They were recruited from the research team’s professional network and from participants in EYLDI’s 2021 study, and each completed an initial screener survey. We sought U.S. leaders who had developed rising leaders within the last three years. Each participant received a $150 Amazon gift card on completing their interview.

  • 17 Leader-Developers interviewed, from 7 U.S. states
  • 100% female; 47% Black, Latina, Asian, or multiracial
  • 65% had 16 or more years in ECE

The rising leaders they described, the Leader-Learners (n=34), were 88% female and 62% Black, Latina, Asian, or multiracial.

2Step 2We interviewed them using Behavioral Event Interviewing

Each interview was a 75-minute structured conversation using a Behavioral Event Interview protocol, an approach built on the idea that past behavior helps predict future performance. It asks people to walk through specific, real situations step by step, not to describe what they believe is good practice. That keeps the focus on what leaders actually did.

Each Leader-Developer described two recent situations in which they were working to develop a Leader-Learner: one they considered effective and successful, and one where they felt frustrated by the outcome. For each, they shared what they said, did, felt, and thought. We asked, but did not require, that at least one story involve a cross-race/ethnicity relationship.

Interviews were held between March and July 2024. Each was recorded, transcribed, and read by members of the research team.

3Step 3We rated the impact of each of the 34 stories

The first review of the data assessed the impact the Leader-Developer had in each story. Every researcher read and rated each story, and then the whole team agreed on a final rating by group consensus. The scale had three points: Most Effective, Effective, or Less Effective. Ratings drew on two criteria:

  • the Leader-Developer’s own perception of their impact, and
  • the team’s assessment of that impact, considering the length of the relationship (for example, whether it ended after a frustrating experience, or the Leader-Learner kept reaching out for support) and the value the Leader-Developer provided.

Because both views counted, the ratings were more evenly spread than a simple split of “effective” and “frustrating” stories would suggest:

Most Effective (6 stories)18%
Effective (17 stories)52%
Less Effective (10 stories)30%

This is not about labeling leaders. It is about clarifying the impact they had in the situations they shared with us.

4Step 4We coded the stories for competencies

To identify what Leader-Developers did to help others become stronger, we used the concept of competencies, first pioneered by David McClelland: the underlying characteristics that predict outstanding impact in a given situation. Some are easily acquired, such as skills and knowledge. Others are deeper, such as traits (e.g. patience), values (e.g. the belief that everyone has the capacity to grow), and motives (e.g. getting satisfaction from helping others grow).

Each competency exists in degrees of complexity and was coded at different levels. For example, Guiding Others was coded at a lower level for giving short-term, job-oriented instruction or demonstration, at a moderate level for customized suggestions or individualized advice, and at a high level for specific positive and/or mixed feedback that enables growth. We also noted missed opportunities, or negative examples, of each competency.

We first coded against the Spencer Competency Dictionary, then refined the competencies to improve how well they accounted for the behaviors that set stories apart in impact. This produced five core competencies: Guiding Others, Understanding Others, Building Relationships, Honoring Identities, and Enabling Others.

5Step 5We compared the most effective stories with the rest

We scored each story for the highest-level behaviors the Leader-Developer showed in each competency. Using these scaled levels, we could assess which competencies were demonstrated at statistically higher levels by leaders achieving Most Effective impact.

That comparison is why the competencies are grouped as they are on this site: Guiding Others was the foundational competency; Understanding Others, Building Relationships, and Honoring Identities worked together to drive strong outcomes; and Enabling Others, less common, was associated with the most effective outcomes.

6Step 6We wrote it up as a Competency Dictionary

For each competency, the Competency Dictionary gives a definition, the behaviors seen at each level, and quotes from the interviews that show the competency in real settings, along with the missed opportunities described above.

The dictionary also includes Managing Performance, a related competency about holding others accountable to a standard of performance.

New to These Terms?

Key terms

A short glossary for readers who haven’t read the report.

ECE
Early care and education: the settings and people who care for and teach young children before kindergarten.
Leader-Developer
A more experienced leader who helps a rising leader grow. In this study, these are the people we interviewed.
Leader-Learner
The rising leader a Leader-Developer is helping to grow.
Developmental relationship
A relationship in which a Leader-Developer helps a Leader-Learner develop skills, navigate career choices, and become a stronger leader.
Competency
An underlying characteristic that predicts outstanding impact in a given situation: a skill, trait, value, or motive.
Level
A step within a competency. Levels deepen in complexity. They are guideposts for growth, not labels for people.
Missed opportunity
A moment where a leader could have shown a competency and did not.
Behavioral Event Interview
An interview method that asks people to describe specific real situations in detail, to learn what they actually did.

Reading the Findings

What this research can and can’t tell us

What it does well

  • Shows what effective leader development looks like in practice, in leaders’ own words.
  • Names specific, learnable behaviors and describes how they deepen over time.
  • Gives leaders and the people who support them a shared language for growth.
  • Builds on competency-based research, an approach used reliably and extensively for more than 50 years.

What to keep in mind

  • Selection bias. Participants came from our network and may have been more interested in developing leaders and/or leadership diversity.
  • One side of the relationship. We interviewed Leader-Developers, not the leaders being developed. Impact was assessed qualitatively, without input from Leader-Learners. We hope to include them in a follow-up study.
  • Behavior, not causes. The approach emphasizes what leaders actually said, felt, and did. It does not show what enabled them to succeed.
  • Limited time. Each 75-minute interview spent about 30 minutes per story and 15 on additional information, so some detail was left out.
  • Timing. The interviews took place in 2024, and COVID-19 was an important backdrop for participants.
  • Small, in-depth sample. This is a qualitative study of 17 leaders’ stories, not a representative sample of all ECE leaders.
  • Levels are guideposts, not a checklist. Growth is rarely linear, and effectiveness depends on context.
